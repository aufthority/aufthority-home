Start here

You already do research. You just don't call it that.

Before buying a new phone, you probably read reviews, asked friends, compared prices, and maybe tried one in the shop. You had a question, gathered information, and made a decision.

Research is that same habit, done carefully and openly, so that other people can check your answer and trust it.

This page walks through what research is, why we do it, and the seven steps every research project follows. No background needed.
What is research?

A careful, organised way of finding answers.

The key word is organised. You decide your question first, plan how you will answer it, collect information in a fair way, and report exactly what you did.

Three ways to answer the same question

A guess

"I think most patients stop their antibiotics early."

Based on a feeling.

An opinion

"My colleagues say patients stop early."

Based on what a few people believe.

Research

"We asked a fair sample of patients the same questions and counted the answers."

Based on a plan anyone can check.

Research does not guarantee the answer is right. It means anyone can see how you got it, and repeat it to check.
Why do research?

Because guessing is expensive.

  • 1To solve real problems. Find out why something goes wrong, so it can be fixed.
  • 2To check what works. Test whether a new treatment, service or idea actually helps.
  • 3To describe what is happening. How common is something, and who does it affect?
  • 4To make better decisions. Doctors, pharmacists and governments choose better with evidence than with habit.
  • 5To add to what we know. Every study is a brick that others can build on.
In health care, a wrong assumption can harm patients. Research replaces "we've always done it this way" with "here is what we found."
Two approaches

Numbers or words? Two ways to find an answer.

Quantitative
Counts and measures

Asks how many, how much, how often.

"What share of patients finish their antibiotic course?"

Uses questionnaires with fixed answers, measurements, and records.

Qualitative
Listens and explores

Asks why and how.

"Why do some patients stop their antibiotics early?"

Uses interviews, group discussions, and open-ended questions.

Many studies use both: numbers show how big a problem is, words explain why it happens. This is called mixed methods.

Try it
Which approach fits each question?
How many hours do students sleep the night before an exam?
Quantitative. It counts hours.
How do new mothers feel about breastfeeding in public?
Qualitative. It explores feelings and experiences.
Is blood pressure lower after a four-week walking programme?
Quantitative. It measures and compares before and after.
  • Descriptive: describes what is there. How common is a problem?
  • Analytical: looks for links. Is one thing connected to another?
  • Experimental: changes something on purpose and watches what happens, like testing a new medicine.
  • Basic vs applied: understanding something for its own sake, or solving a practical problem.
Quality

What makes research good, and what ruins it.

Good research
  • A clear, focused question
  • Planned before any data is collected
  • A fair way of choosing who takes part
  • Honest reporting, even of disappointing results
  • Enough detail for someone else to repeat it
  • Ethical: people agree to take part and are protected
Bad research
  • A vague question, like "a study about health"
  • Changing the plan after seeing the results
  • Only asking people likely to agree with you
  • Hiding results that don't fit
  • Too little detail to check or repeat
  • Collecting data without permission
How it's done

Every research project follows the same journey.

The details change from study to study, but the route stays the same.

Our example for the next seven steps

A pharmacist notices that some patients come back with the same infection a week later. She suspects they stopped taking their antibiotics too early. Is she right? Let's research it.

This is a made-up example, used only to show the steps.

Step 1 of 7 · Spot a problem

Start with a problem worth solving.

A research problem is a gap between what is happening and what should happen, where nobody yet has a clear answer.

Before you pick one, ask

  • 1Does it matter? Does it affect many people, or cause harm?
  • 2Is it already answered? If so, read the answer instead.
  • 3Can I actually do it? Think about time, money, and access to people.
  • 4Is it ethical? Will anyone be put at risk?
  • 5Will the answer be useful? Can someone act on it?

In our example: from problem to title

Problem
Patients return with the same infection.
↓
Question
Do patients at our clinic finish their antibiotic course, and if not, why?
↓
Title
Completion of antibiotic courses, and reasons for stopping early, among outpatients at a community clinic
A good title tells the reader what, who and where, in one line.
Step 2 of 7 · See what's known

Find out what others have already found.

This is called a literature review: reading earlier studies on your topic. It stops you repeating old work, shows how others measured the same thing, and reveals what is still missing.

Primary source

The original study, written by the people who did it.

e.g. a journal article reporting a survey

Secondary source

Someone summarising or discussing other people's studies.

e.g. textbooks, review articles, news stories

How to search

  • 1Pick keywords: antibiotic, adherence, completion, outpatient.
  • 2Search trusted places: Google Scholar, PubMed, your library.
  • 3Read the summary first (the abstract) and keep only relevant studies.
  • 4Note what each found, and what it didn't look at.
  • 5Save the full reference for every source you use.
In our example

She finds studies from other countries, but few from clinics like hers, and almost none that asked patients why they stopped. That gap is what her study will fill.

A good review doesn't just list studies. It connects them and points to the gap your study fills.
Step 3 of 7 · Set your aim

Decide exactly what you want to find out.

An objective is a clear statement of what your study will do. It keeps you focused and tells you what data to collect.

General objective: the big aim

To study how well outpatients at Clinic X complete their antibiotic course.

Specific objectives: the measurable steps

1. To determine the proportion of patients who complete their course.

2. To identify the reasons patients give for stopping early.

3. To compare completion between younger and older patients.

Weak

"To understand antibiotic use."

Understand what, exactly? How would you know you've done it?

Strong

Starts with an action you can check:

determine, identify, compare, measure, describe.

Your objectives become the backbone of your research proposal, the written plan for your whole study. We put it all together after Step 7.
Step 4 of 7 · Choose what to measure

Name the things you will measure.

A variable is anything that can change from one person to another, like age, a test score, or whether someone finished their medicine.

Independent
The factors you think make a difference
Age
Understanding of instructions
Side effects
→
Dependent
The outcome you measure
Finished the course?
(yes or no)

Two kinds of variable

Categorical

Fits into groups. Yes or no, male or female, age group.

Numerical

Is a number you can count or measure. Age in years, doses missed.

Watch for hidden factors. If older patients finish their course more often, is it their age, or is it that they see the pharmacist more often? A factor that affects both sides can fool you. Researchers call it a confounding variable.
Step 5 of 7 · Collect the data

Choose who to ask, and how to ask them.

Data can be primary (you collect it yourself) or secondary (it already exists, like hospital records or national surveys).

Questionnaire Interview Observation Records review Measurements

Why you don't need to ask everyone

You don't drink the whole pot to know if soup needs salt. You stir it well and taste a spoonful. The pot is the population. The spoonful is your sample. Stirring is choosing people fairly.
Probability sampling

Everyone has a known chance of being picked, for example a random draw from the patient list.

Best for saying something about everyone.

Non-probability sampling

People are picked by convenience or judgement, for example whoever walks in today.

Easier, but may not represent everyone.

The trap in our example

If she only surveys patients who come back to the pharmacy, she misses the ones who stopped early and never returned. Her results would look better than reality. This is called sampling bias.

The fix: pick patients at random from the prescription records and contact each one.

Step 6 of 7 · Make sense of it

Clean up the data, then find the story.

  • 1Sort it. Put every answer into one table, label it clearly, and give each person a code instead of a name.
  • 2Check it. Look for missing answers, impossible values (an age of 250), and duplicates.
  • 3Spot answers you can't trust. Some forms are filled in carelessly.
  • 4Analyse it. For numbers: count, work out percentages, compare groups. For words: read through and group similar answers into themes.

Answers that need a second look

Same answer every time

Ticks "agree" for every single question.

Too fast

Finishes a 15-minute form in one minute.

Mostly blank

Skips most of the questions.

Contradicts itself

"Never missed a dose" and "missed three doses."

In our example

When she groups the reasons patients gave, three themes stand out: "I felt better already", "side effects" and "I forgot."

Made-up result, for illustration.

Step 7 of 7 · Share it

Share it, so others can check it and use it.

Research that stays in a drawer helps nobody. Sharing lets others check your method, use your findings, and build on them.

What a research presentation covers

  • 1Title: what, who and where.
  • 2Introduction: the problem and why it matters.
  • 3Literature review: what is already known, and the gap.
  • 4Methodology: who you asked, what you measured, and how.
  • 5Citations and references: credit for every source you used.
  • 6Appendix: extras like your questionnaire and consent form.
The end is also a beginning. Her finding raises a new question: would a simple reminder text help patients finish their antibiotics? That's the next study. Research is a cycle.
Putting it together · The research proposal

A proposal is your whole plan, written down before you start.

An architect draws the building before anyone pours concrete. A research proposal does the same for a study: it shows your supervisor, or an ethics committee, exactly what you plan to do, so mistakes are caught on paper instead of after months of work.

When do you write it? After you've worked through Steps 1 to 4, and before you collect any data. It describes what you will do, so it's written in the future tense: "We will survey…", not "We found…".

Common mistakes
  • Objectives and methods don't match
  • The literature review is a list of summaries, with no gap
  • Writing results before collecting any data
  • A vague sample: "some patients" instead of who, how many and how chosen
  • Missing or inconsistent references
The one-minute check

Read each objective, then look for the method that answers it.

Every objective needs a matching method. If one doesn't have one, something is missing from your plan.

Spot the mistake
A student's proposal says:
Objective: to compare antibiotic completion between younger and older patients.
Method: we will survey patients aged 60 and above.
The method can't answer the objective. You can't compare younger and older patients if you only ask older ones. The sample must include both age groups.
