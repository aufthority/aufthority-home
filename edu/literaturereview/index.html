Literature reviews

Nobody can read everything.

So how do you find out what is already known about a question, fairly and without picking only the studies you like? That is the job of a literature review.

What a review is

A woven answer, not a pile of summaries.

A weak review lists studies one by one. A good one pulls them together into themes: what keeps coming up, where studies disagree, and what nobody has looked at yet.

The process

Seven steps, one funnel.

Every review moves from a big messy pile to a small set of studies you can trust, and then to one clear story.

Narrative reviews follow these steps loosely. Scoping and systematic reviews follow them strictly and write every step down, so someone else could repeat them.

Step 1 · Ask a clear question

A vague question finds a vague pile.

"Antibiotics and patients" could mean anything. Break your question into parts with PICO. Tap each letter.

Mapping a topic rather than testing something? Use PCC instead: Population, Concept, Context.

In adults prescribed antibiotics, does a pharmacist reminder call, compared with usual care, help them finish the full course?

Each letter becomes a search concept in Step 2.

Example question
Step 2 · Choose your words

Three little words control the pile.

AND narrows, OR widens, NOT removes. Tap each to see what a database would bring back.

(adherence OR compliance OR "course completion") AND antibiotic*

Use OR between synonyms. A star finds every ending: antibiotic* catches antibiotic and antibiotics.

Step 3 · Search the right places

One database is never enough.

Each database covers different journals. Then follow the trail: check what your best papers cite, and who cited them later. This is called snowballing.

Google ScholarWide but messy. For checking.
Grey literatureTheses, reports, trial registers.
Step 4 · Sort the pile

From hundreds of hits to a handful that fit.

Decide what counts before you look. Write down who, what and which designs you'll include.
Sort in stages. Titles and abstracts first, then read the survivors in full.
Use two people, working separately, then compare and settle disagreements.

Step 5 · Judge quality

Not every study deserves the same trust.

This is critical appraisal: checking how a study was done, not whether you like its answer. Tap each feature. Would it make you trust the result more, or less?

Step 6 · Pull out the facts

Same questions, asked of every study.

Build a table before you start reading. Fill in the same columns for every included study, so nothing important slips through and studies can be compared side by side.

Step 7 · Weave it together

This is the part readers actually want.

Synthesis means organising by idea, not by study. Say where studies agree, where they clash, how strong the evidence is, and what's missing.

Review type 1

Narrative review: an expert's guided tour.

AsksWhat do we know about why patients stop antibiotics early?
HowAn expert reads widely, picks key studies, groups them by theme and gives a view.
Good forIntroducing a topic, textbook chapters, proposing a new idea.
Watch outThe author chooses what goes in, so it can lean towards their own view. Say how you searched.
Check withSANRA, a six-item quality scale for narrative reviews.

Review type 2

Scoping review: a map of the evidence.

AsksWhat has been studied on this, in whom, where and how, and what's missing?
HowSearch widely using PCC, then chart the key features of every source into a table or map.
Good forNew or messy topics, and checking whether a systematic review is worth doing.
Watch outIt maps; it doesn't say what works. Quality appraisal is usually optional.
Report withPRISMA-ScR. Register the plan on OSF: PROSPERO doesn't accept scoping reviews.

Review type 3

Systematic review: strict, planned, checkable.

AsksDo pharmacist reminder calls help patients finish their antibiotics?
HowPlan first and register the plan. Search several databases. Two people screen and extract. Appraise every study.
Good forGuiding practice and policy, because anyone can check and repeat it.
Watch outIt takes a team and many months. Narrow, one-database searches don't count.
Report withPRISMA 2020. Register on PROSPERO before data extraction starts.

Summary

Six things to remember.

1A review weaves studies together. It doesn't just list them.
2Start with a clear, searchable question, using PICO or PCC.
3Search more than one place, and write down exactly how.
4Decide what counts before you look, then sort the pile in stages.
5Judge each study's quality before you trust its result.
6Pick the review type that fits your question, then follow its checklist.
Keep going
