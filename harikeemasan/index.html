Proof it can be done

Some places have many people who live to 100.

They are called Blue Zones. Scientists do not take ages on trust: each person's age is checked against birth, marriage and family records. (Poulain et al., 2013; Poulain & Herm, 2025)

Okinawa, JapanSardinia, ItalyIkaria, GreeceNicoya, Costa RicaMartinique, France
What they share

Four habits Blue Zones have in common.

None of them need a gym or a prescription. (Poulain & Herm, 2025)

A warning

Habits that stop, benefits that fade.

Even in Blue Zones, traditional meals are giving way to modern fast food. Researchers warn the long-life advantage cannot be taken for granted. (Pes et al., 2022)

Honest note: research on Blue Zones is still growing, and scientists are still weighing how strong the evidence is. (Candal-Pedreira et al., 2026)

How we compare

Where do the 100-year-olds live today?

Malaysia: 4,287 citizens aged 100+. (DOSM, June 2025)

Battery 1 · Food

Quarter, quarter, half.

Malaysia's Healthy Plate: half vegetables and fruit, a quarter rice or other grains, a quarter fish, chicken, meat or legumes such as tofu and dhal, with plain water. (NHMS 2024)

Yet only 12% of Malaysian adults eat 3 servings of vegetables a day, and under 3% eat a daily serving of nuts or legumes. (NHMS 2024, adults 18+) Blue Zone meals look much the same: mostly plants. (Pes et al., 2022)

Battery 1 · Malaysians aged 60+

1 in 4 older Malaysians eat out of balance.

The 2024 national survey found most people aged 60 and over were not eating enough of several nutrients, while 64% ate too much fat. (NHMS 2024)

Based on what people said they ate, not blood tests.

Battery 1 · Myth buster

Does soy cause cancer? Not in these studies.

A common fear is that tofu and soy milk cause cancer. In a study of 73,223 women in Shanghai, eating more soy foods was linked to a lower, not higher, risk of breast cancer, mainly before menopause. The link was strongest in women who ate soy as teenagers and as adults. (Lee et al., 2009)

So there is no need to fear tofu and tempeh. They can stay on the family table, alongside fish and chicken.

Battery 1 · Hidden salt and sugar

Too salty, too sweet.

3 in 4 Malaysian adults eat too much salt, which raises blood pressure: 7.3 g a day against a limit under 5 g (1 teaspoon). 1 in 2 eat over 7.5 teaspoons of sugar a day, half from drinks. (NHMS 2024, adults 18+)

40% of people aged 60+ never read food labels. (NHMS 2024)

Battery 3 · A free tool

MyUBAT: your medicines, on your phone.

If you get medicines from a Ministry of Health clinic or hospital, the free MyUBAT app shows all your prescriptions, how much medicine you have left, and your next appointments. (MyUBAT, KKM)

At taking-part facilities, you can collect at the counter, by post, at a drive-through or from a medicine locker. Carers can manage several accounts on one phone. In BM, English, Mandarin and Tamil.

Battery 4 · Supplements

Supplements count as medicines too.

56.9% of older adults in the same Malaysian study took supplements, adding a lot to their total pill count. (Lim et al., 2017)

About 1 in 2 older Malaysians use traditional or complementary medicine. Mixed with medicines without enough knowledge, these can cause serious side effects. (Chang et al., 2021)

Take it home

A daily card for you and your carer.

Take a screenshot to keep it on your phone.

  • Check your pillbox against your medicine list.
  • Stretch gently or take a short walk in the morning sun.
  • Add vegetables or ulam to your first meal if you can.
  • Fill half your plate with vegetables or ulam.
  • Do your strength or balance exercise.
  • Spend time with people, or on something that gives you purpose.
  • Check your evening medicines and supplements.
  • Switch on the night light and clear the path to the bathroom.
  • Put non-slip slippers by the bed.
  • Refill your pillbox with a family member checking the labels.
  • Check MyUBAT for your medicine balance and next appointment.
  • Join a PAWE activity or meet friends.
Your turn

Live long. Stay independent.

  1. A long, healthy life can be measured, and it is kept up by daily habits.
  2. Food: quarter, quarter, half; less salt and sugar; soy is linked to lower, not higher, cancer risk.
  3. Movement: move daily, build strength and balance, and make your home safe.
  4. Medicines and supplements: know what you take, review it with a pharmacist, and use MyUBAT and NPRA's checks.

About this page

A plain-language companion to the talk Facing the Golden Years in Good Health (USIM x PAWE, 21 October 2026), written for older adults, carers and the public. It explains research simply and is not a substitute for advice from your own doctor or pharmacist. Characters and homes are made up.
